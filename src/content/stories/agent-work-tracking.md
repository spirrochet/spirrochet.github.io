When I decided to get back into hands-on development, I also decided to do it with AI coding agents.
I'd read plenty of claims about what these tools can and can't do. I wanted evidence, and I wanted it to be mine.

So every session gets written up: what I was trying to do, what actually happened, what it cost, and
what went wrong or right. Even the ordinary ones, where nothing much happens. Especially those, really.
They're the only way to tell how much of working with an agent is routine and how much is the occasional
surprise. And if you only write down the sessions that went spectacularly well or badly, you end up
believing both stories and learning from neither.

The notebook stays private. What I share here is only what's still true and useful with every name,
machine, and number taken out. Otherwise it stays in the notebook.

A few things have stood out so far:

- **Writing a lesson down doesn't stop the same mistake happening again.** Turning it into a test that
  runs every time, and fails when the mistake comes back, does.
- **Missing isn't the same as empty.** When information doesn't come back, an agent will often treat it
  as "nothing there", and say so confidently.
- **A checklist gets done the cheapest way that technically satisfies it.** So the checklist has to say
  what done looks like, not just name the thing to do.
- **Measuring beats predicting.** I tried two rules of thumb for what a session would cost. Both fitted
  for a while, then both broke.

The experiment runs until early 2027. I'll keep adding what holds up.
